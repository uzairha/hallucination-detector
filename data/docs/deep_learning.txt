DEEP LEARNING: NEURAL NETWORK BASICS

Deep learning uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn representations from data. A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and applies an activation function. Without non-linear activation functions, a stack of linear layers is equivalent to a single linear transformation. The universal approximation theorem states that a feedforward network with a single hidden layer and enough units can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain to arbitrary accuracy. The theorem says nothing about how to find those weights or how many units are needed.

Common activation functions include ReLU (rectified linear unit), which is max(0, x); sigmoid, which maps to (0, 1); tanh, which maps to (-1, 1); and GELU and SiLU/Swish, which are smooth variants used in many transformers. Softmax converts a vector of scores into a probability distribution that sums to 1 and is typically used in the output layer for multi-class classification.

DEEP LEARNING: TRAINING

Training minimizes a loss function. Mean squared error is common for regression, and cross-entropy loss is common for classification. Backpropagation computes the gradients of the loss with respect to all parameters using the chain rule. Gradient descent updates parameters in the direction opposite to the gradient, scaled by the learning rate. A common misconception is that backpropagation is the optimization algorithm. Backpropagation computes gradients, and an optimizer such as SGD or Adam uses them to update the weights.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) updates parameters using mini-batches of data. Momentum accelerates SGD by accumulating past gradients. Adam combines momentum with per-parameter adaptive learning rates. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update and is widely used for training transformers. A learning rate that is too high can cause divergence, and one that is too low makes training slow. Learning rate schedules such as warmup and cosine decay are common.

An epoch is one full pass over the training data. A batch is the subset of examples used to compute one gradient update, and an iteration or step is one such update.

The vanishing gradient problem occurs when gradients shrink as they are propagated back through many layers, which slows learning in early layers. It is common with sigmoid and tanh activations in deep or recurrent networks. Exploding gradients are the opposite and can be controlled with gradient clipping. ReLU activations, careful weight initialization (such as Xavier/Glorot or He initialization), residual connections, and normalization layers help with these problems.

DEEP LEARNING: REGULARIZATION AND NORMALIZATION

Dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of activations during training to reduce overfitting, and it is turned off during inference. Batch normalization normalizes activations across the batch dimension. Layer normalization normalizes across the features of each individual example, which suits sequence models and variable batch sizes. Transformers typically use layer normalization or RMSNorm. Early stopping halts training when validation performance stops improving. Data augmentation creates modified copies of training examples, such as image flips and crops.

DEEP LEARNING: ARCHITECTURES

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) use learned filters that slide over the input and share weights, and pooling layers reduce spatial resolution. CNNs are well suited to images and other grid-like data. AlexNet's win in the 2012 ImageNet challenge popularized deep CNNs. ResNet, introduced in 2015, used residual (skip) connections to train much deeper networks.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences step by step while maintaining a hidden state. Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997, use gates to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem. Gated recurrent units (GRUs) are a simpler gated variant. RNNs process tokens sequentially, which limits parallelism during training. Transformers replaced recurrence with attention to remove that limitation.

Autoencoders learn to compress and reconstruct their input. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) learn a probabilistic latent space and can generate new samples. Generative adversarial networks (GANs), introduced by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues in 2014, train a generator and a discriminator against each other. Diffusion models generate data by learning to reverse a gradual noising process and are used in image generators such as Stable Diffusion.

Transfer learning reuses a model trained on one task as the starting point for another. Fine-tuning continues training a pretrained model on task-specific data.

DEEP LEARNING: HARDWARE AND FRAMEWORKS

Deep learning training relies heavily on GPUs and other accelerators, such as Google's TPUs, because neural network computations are dominated by highly parallel matrix multiplications. PyTorch, originally developed at Meta AI, and TensorFlow, developed at Google, are the most widely used deep learning frameworks. JAX is a Google library for high-performance numerical computing with automatic differentiation. Training usually uses mixed precision, such as FP16 or BF16, to reduce memory use and increase speed. Quantization reduces the numerical precision of weights, for example to 8-bit or 4-bit integers, to shrink models and speed up inference, usually at some cost in accuracy.
